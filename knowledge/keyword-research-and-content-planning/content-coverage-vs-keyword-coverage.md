Two terms sound almost the same and get mixed up all the time: **content
coverage** and **keyword coverage**. They measure two different
things, and a page needs both.[^tanveer-coverage]

> [!TIP]
> The one line to remember: **content coverage is about ideas, keyword
> coverage is about words.**

## Content coverage: did I answer everything?

Content coverage is **how completely your page answers everything a reader
wants to know** about the topic.[^tanveer-coverage]

Say you build a **BMI calculator** page. A visitor arrives expecting answers
to:

1. What is BMI?
2. How do I check my BMI?
3. What is the BMI formula?
4. What do the results mean?
5. How can I improve my BMI?

Answer all five and your content coverage is strong. Put up only the
calculator, with nothing around it, and your coverage is
weak.[^tanveer-coverage]

## Keyword coverage: did I use the words people type?

Keyword coverage is **how many of the important search phrases people type
into Google you have used naturally** on the page.[^tanveer-coverage]

For the same BMI page, people search things like:

- `BMI calculator`
- `BMI calculator for men`
- `BMI calculator for women`
- `calculate BMI online`
- `BMI formula`
- `free BMI calculator`

If these phrases appear naturally across your page, your keyword coverage
is strong.[^tanveer-coverage]

## Side by side

The two, compared:[^tanveer-coverage]

| | Content coverage | Keyword coverage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The question it asks | Did I answer all the important questions about this topic? | Did I use the important phrases people actually search? |
| It is about | Ideas | Words |
| Weak when | The page skips something the reader came to learn | The page never uses the phrases people type |

## A second example: how to make tea

**Content coverage**, the article explains:[^tanveer-coverage]

- what ingredients you need,
- the step-by-step method,
- how long to boil the tea,
- how much sugar to add.

**Keyword coverage**, the article naturally uses:

- `how to make tea`
- `tea recipe`
- `milk tea recipe`
- `easy tea recipe`

## Why you need both

- **Good content, no keywords**: the page may never show up for the
  searches people actually make.[^tanveer-coverage]
- **Lots of keywords, thin content**: the page does not help the reader,
  and Google will not rank it well.

> [!WARNING]
> Never repeat a keyword again and again just to "cover" it. Write useful,
> complete, natural content, and place keywords where they fit.

## Practice exercise

**Topic: "Best Calculator for Students"**

**Step 1: check your content coverage.** Does your article explain:

- [ ] types of calculators (basic, scientific, graphing)
- [ ] which type fits which class level
- [ ] key features to look for
- [ ] the price range
- [ ] how to choose the right one

**Step 2: check your keyword coverage.** Did you naturally use phrases
like:

- [ ] `calculator for students`
- [ ] `best calculator for school`
- [ ] `online student calculator`
- [ ] `scientific calculator for students`

**Step 3: score yourself.** Count how many items you covered in each
step.[^tanveer-coverage]

### Quick test: content problem or keyword problem?

| Sentence | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "My article never explains how to choose a calculator." | Content coverage |
| "My article explains everything, but I never wrote the phrase 'scientific calculator for students'." | Keyword coverage |

## How NicheSpark helps with keyword coverage

Keyword coverage depends on knowing **the phrases people actually type**.
Google's autocomplete dropdown shows exactly those phrases, and
[NicheSpark](nichespark-autocomplete-keyword-tool.md) is a free Chrome
extension that collects them for you: it types each query into Google,
waits for the suggestions, and saves every one with the query that found
it.[^nichespark-cws]

How to use it for a page like the BMI calculator:

1. **Create a project** with the page's main keyword as the seed, for
   example `bmi calculator`, and pick your Google domain (google.com.pk,
   google.co.uk and others are supported).
2. **Run A to Z** to see the breadth of the topic (26 queries, `bmi
   calculator a` to `z`).
3. **Run suffixes** for the variations people add after the seed, for
   example `for men`, `for women`, `online`, `formula`.
4. **Run prefixes** for words people put before it, for example `free` or
   `best`.
5. **Export the list** (CSV or Copy) and tick off, one by one, which
   phrases your page already uses naturally. The ones missing are your
   keyword coverage gaps.

It helps with **content coverage** too. Autocomplete often returns whole
questions, and a **template** such as `how much does {seed} cost` lets you
probe for them directly. Each question you collect is a candidate section
your page should answer.

Keep its limits in mind, as its own guide states:

- It shows **no search volume or difficulty**, so check the important
  phrases in a metrics tool before you plan around them.
- Results change with location and language, so research in the country
  you are targeting.
- It only finds the phrases. Writing them in **naturally**, without
  stuffing, is still your job, as the warning above says.
